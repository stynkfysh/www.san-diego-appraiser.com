Date of Death Appraisals Across San Diego County

When a homeowner passes away, the market value of the house on that exact date becomes a number that follows the family for years. It sets the new tax basis for the heirs, it goes into the trust accounting, and it may be examined by the IRS or a probate examiner. A retrospective appraisal is how that number gets documented properly.

Who usually orders one

Successor trustees are the most frequent clients, followed by probate attorneys, CPAs preparing a final return or Form 706, and siblings who want a neutral figure before one of them buys out the others. Some families call within weeks of a death; others call years later when the house is finally sold and the accountant asks what the basis was. Both situations are routine.

Looking backward, not at today's market

The analysis relies on sales that closed before and around the date of death, adjusted for the market conditions that existed then, not on what similar homes fetch this month. If the house has since been cleaned out, repaired or remodeled, the report describes it as it stood on the effective date, using photographs, listing history, permits and family recollection where needed.

Why the basis matters so much here

Many San Diego County homes were purchased decades ago at a fraction of their current value. Because inherited property generally receives a basis equal to its value at death, a well-supported appraisal can shrink the taxable gain on a later sale dramatically. An unsupported or missing value leaves heirs exposed if the figure is ever questioned.

What you receive

A signed, USPAP-compliant report stating the effective date, the property rights appraised, the comparable evidence and the reasoning that connects them. It is written so that a trust officer, an IRS reviewer or a judge can follow it without a phone call. Most single-family assignments use the Standard or Desktop tier on the fee schedule.
